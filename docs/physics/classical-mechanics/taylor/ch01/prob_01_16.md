# Problem 1.16

## Problem Statement

**(a)** Defining the scalar product $\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}$ by Equation (1.7),

$$
\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s} = \sum_{i} r_i s_i = r_x s_x + r_y s_y + r_z s_z
$$

show that Pythagoras's theorem implies that the magnitude of any vector $\mathbf{r}$ is $r = \sqrt{\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{r}}$.

**(b)** It is clear that the length of a vector does not depend on our choice of coordinate axes. Thus the result of part (a) guarantees that the scalar product $\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{r}$, as defined by (1.7), is the same for any choice of orthogonal axes. Use this to prove that $\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}$, as defined by (1.7), is the same for any choice of orthogonal axes.

[*Hint:* Consider the length of the vector $\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}$.]

---

## Solution

### Part (a)

Using the component definition of the scalar product (1.7) for any vector $\mathbf{r} = (r_x, r_y, r_z)$ evaluated with itself:

$$
\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{r} = \sum_{i=1}^{3} r_i^2 = r_x^2 + r_y^2 + r_z^2
$$

By the three-dimensional Pythagorean theorem (established in Problem 1.6), the spatial length $r = |\mathbf{r}|$ of a vector is the space diagonal of a rectangular box with orthogonal side lengths $|r_x|$, $|r_y|$, and $|r_z|$:

$$
r^2 = r_x^2 + r_y^2 + r_z^2
$$

Equating the two expressions:

$$
r^2 = \mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{r} \implies r = \sqrt{\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{r}}
$$

---

### Part (b)

The spatial length of any physical vector is an intrinsic geometric quantity that is completely independent of the choice of orthogonal coordinate axes. Consequently, part (a) establishes that:
- $\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{r} = r^2$ is invariant under any rotation or reflection of orthogonal axes.
- $\mathbf{s} \cdot \mathbf{s} = s^2$ is likewise invariant.

Now consider the sum vector $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}$. Because $\mathbf{w}$ is itself a physical vector, its squared length $|\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}|^2$ is also invariant under any choice of orthogonal axes:

$$
|\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}|^2 = (\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}) \cdot (\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s})
$$

Expanding this scalar product using definition (1.7):

$$
(\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}) \cdot (\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}) = \sum_{i=1}^{3} (r_i + s_i)(r_i + s_i) = \sum_{i=1}^{3} \left( r_i^2 + 2 r_i s_i + s_i^2 \right)
$$

Distributing the summation over the terms:

$$
(\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}) \cdot (\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}) = \sum_{i=1}^{3} r_i^2 + 2 \sum_{i=1}^{3} r_i s_i + \sum_{i=1}^{3} s_i^2 = (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{r}) + 2(\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}) + (\mathbf{s} \cdot \mathbf{s})
$$

Isolating $\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}$:

$$
\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ |\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}|^2 - (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{r}) - (\mathbf{s} \cdot \mathbf{s}) \right]
$$

or, expressing each term purely in terms of vector lengths:

$$
\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s} = \frac{1}{2} \left( |\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}|^2 - |\mathbf{r}|^2 - |\mathbf{s}|^2 \right)
$$

Since each term on the right-hand side ($|\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}|$, $|\mathbf{r}|$, and $|\mathbf{s}|$) is a coordinate-independent physical length, their combination is also strictly invariant.

Therefore, the scalar product $\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}$, as defined by (1.7), takes the exact same numerical value for any choice of orthogonal axes.
