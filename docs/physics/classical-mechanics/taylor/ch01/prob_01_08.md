# Problem 1.8

!!! info "Textbook Reference"
    - **Textbook**: *Classical Mechanics* (John R. Taylor)
    - **Chapter**: 1 — Newton's Laws of Motion
    - **Problem**: 1.8
    - **Topic**: Properties of the Scalar Product (Distributivity and Product Rule)

---

## Problem Statement

**(a)** Use the coordinate definition of the scalar product,

$$
\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s} = r_x s_x + r_y s_y + r_z s_z = \sum_{i=1}^3 r_i s_i
$$

to prove that the scalar product is distributive over vector addition:

$$
\mathbf{r} \cdot (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}
$$

**(b)** If $\mathbf{r}(t)$ and $\mathbf{s}(t)$ are vectors that depend on time, prove that the product rule for differentiation applies to their scalar product:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}) = \mathbf{r} \cdot \frac{d\mathbf{s}}{dt} + \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} \cdot \mathbf{s}
$$

---

## Solution

### Part (a): Distributivity of the Scalar Product

Let Cartesian components of the vectors be given by:

$$
\mathbf{r} = (r_x, r_y, r_z), \quad \mathbf{u} = (u_x, u_y, u_z), \quad \mathbf{v} = (v_x, v_y, v_z)
$$

The sum vector $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ has components:

$$
w_i = u_i + v_i \quad (i = x, y, z)
$$

Applying the coordinate definition of the scalar product:

$$
\mathbf{r} \cdot (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \sum_{i=1}^3 r_i (u_i + v_i)
$$

By the distributive property of standard real-number multiplication:

$$
r_i (u_i + v_i) = r_i u_i + r_i v_i
$$

Substituting this into the summation:

$$
\mathbf{r} \cdot (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \sum_{i=1}^3 (r_i u_i + r_i v_i) = \sum_{i=1}^3 r_i u_i + \sum_{i=1}^3 r_i v_i
$$

Recognizing each sum as the definition of the respective dot product yields:

$$
\mathbf{r} \cdot (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}
$$

---

### Part (b): Product Rule for Differentiation

Express the scalar product $\mathbf{r}(t) \cdot \mathbf{s}(t)$ in terms of Cartesian components:

$$
\mathbf{r}(t) \cdot \mathbf{s}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^3 r_i(t) s_i(t)
$$

Differentiating both sides with respect to time $t$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}) = \frac{d}{dt} \left( \sum_{i=1}^3 r_i(t) s_i(t) \right) = \sum_{i=1}^3 \frac{d}{dt} [r_i(t) s_i(t)]
$$

Applying the ordinary single-variable calculus product rule to each component product $r_i(t) s_i(t)$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} [r_i(t) s_i(t)] = r_i(t) \frac{ds_i}{dt} + \frac{dr_i}{dt} s_i(t)
$$

Substituting this back into the sum:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}) = \sum_{i=1}^3 \left( r_i(t) \frac{ds_i}{dt} + \frac{dr_i}{dt} s_i(t) \right) = \sum_{i=1}^3 r_i(t) \frac{ds_i}{dt} + \sum_{i=1}^3 \frac{dr_i}{dt} s_i(t)
$$

Recognizing the first term as $\mathbf{r} \cdot \frac{d\mathbf{s}}{dt}$ and the second term as $\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} \cdot \mathbf{s}$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}) = \mathbf{r} \cdot \frac{d\mathbf{s}}{dt} + \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} \cdot \mathbf{s}
$$
